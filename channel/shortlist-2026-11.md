## 1. Deptford 1905: the thumbprint on the cash box
- **Title idea:** The Thumbprint That Hanged Two Brothers (Deptford, 1905)
- **Summary:** Thomas and Ann Farrow were attacked in their shop in Deptford. A greasy thumbprint on
  the emptied cash box was matched to Alfred Stratton. Alfred and Albert Stratton were convicted.
- **Why:** The first murder conviction in Britain on fingerprint evidence; the defence called a
  fingerprint expert who had offered to testify for either side.
- **Distinct:** Courtroom fight over whether fingerprints were science at all.
- **Sources:** Wikipedia "Stratton Brothers case" (saved in `videos/deptford/`); Old Bailey
  Proceedings, May 1905; Metropolitan Police history pages.
- **Length / risk:** 20 min. Low risk: historical, convicted, no graphic material needed.
- **Priority:** 1. Groundwork is done, and it continues the "forensic first" thread from Crippen.

## 2. Brides in the Bath, 1915
- **Title idea:** Three Brides, Three Baths: How Spilsbury Proved Murder (1915)
- **Summary:** George Joseph Smith married women under false names; three died in baths. Bernard
  Spilsbury showed how a drowning could leave no marks.
- **Why:** Very strong story; the judge allowed evidence of the other deaths to show a system.
- **Distinct:** Links to Crippen (Spilsbury testified there too), so it builds a channel thread.
- **Sources:** Wikipedia "George Joseph Smith"; Old Bailey Proceedings 1915; Notable British Trials
  volume (1918). Spilsbury's reliability is debated by later historians: mark as contested.
- **Length / risk:** 20 min. Low-medium: deaths of women, handled without detail.
- **Priority:** 2. More competition than Deptford.

## 3. PC George Gutteridge, 1927
- **Title idea:** The Bullet That Named Two Killers (Essex, 1927)
- **Summary:** An Essex police constable was shot on a country lane. Frederick Browne and William
  Kennedy were convicted after a gunsmith matched the cartridge to Browne's revolver.
- **Why:** One of the first British trials to rest on firearms identification; a policeman victim.
- **Distinct:** Little competition on YouTube (to be confirmed).
- **Sources:** Wikipedia "Murder of George Gutteridge"; Essex Police Museum; trial reports.
  The popular claim that his eyes were shot because of the "image on the retina" belief is
  disputed: mark as contested.
- **Length / risk:** 20 min. Low.
- **Priority:** 3. Strong for originality, lower search demand.

## 4. Alfred Rouse and the blazing car, 1931
- **Title idea:** The Man in the Burning Car Has Never Been Named
- **Summary:** Rouse set fire to his car with an unknown man inside and was convicted of murder.
  The victim has never been formally identified.
- **Why:** A closed case with an open question at its centre.
- **Sources:** Wikipedia "Alfred Rouse"; Notable British Trials. Later DNA work on the victim's
  identity is to be verified before it is used.
- **Length / risk:** 20 min. Low-medium: fire death.
- **Priority:** 4.

## 5. Buck Ruxton, 1935
- **Title idea:** How a Photograph Identified the Victims (Lancaster, 1935)
- **Summary:** A Lancaster doctor killed his partner Isabella and the maid Mary Rogerson. Glasgow
  scientists matched a skull to a portrait photograph.
- **Why:** Landmark in forensic identification.
- **Sources:** Wikipedia "Buck Ruxton"; Glaister and Brash, *Medico-legal Aspects of the Ruxton
  Case* (1937).
- **Risk:** Medium for advertisers: dismemberment. Needs a restrained script.
- **Priority:** 5.

## 6. Marie Lafarge, 1840 (France)
- **Title idea:** The Arsenic Trial That Put Chemistry in the Courtroom (1840)
- **Summary:** Marie Lafarge was convicted of poisoning her husband after Mathieu Orfila's Marsh
  test found arsenic.
- **Why:** First international case for the channel; widely followed by the press at the time.
- **Distinct:** Guilt is still debated: the video can lay out both sides.
- **Sources:** Wikipedia "Marie Lafarge"; her *Mémoires* (1841); contemporary trial reports.
- **Risk:** Low.
- **Priority:** 6. Good test of whether the audience follows the channel outside Britain.
