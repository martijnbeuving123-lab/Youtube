# Script — The Duquesne Spy Ring (The Defector Files)

**Angle (originality rule 1):** the ring's greatest coup, the Norden bombsight, had already left
New York in an umbrella in January 1938, two years before the FBI's case began; so this is not the
story of a secret saved but of the first time the FBI took over an enemy service's own channel (a
coerced recruit, a consulate visit, a radio Hamburg trusted for 16 months), and of how the man who
made it possible was written out of the legend.

**Analysis passages (rule 2):** beats 26-27 (why coercion fails), 37-38 (a channel, not a spy), 46-47 (legend
versus file), 69-71 (what the Norden theft achieved: disputed), 91-92 (who gets the credit), 93-95
(method; MI5's parallel system and Ritter beaten twice).

**Opening (rule 3):** scene-first cold open behind the two-way mirror (no date lead, unlike the
Rosenberg pilot's "On the evening of June nineteenth, 1953..."), then the umbrella twist as the
stakes line.

## Script

(Beats split so no visual stays on screen much longer than 20 s; `orig` = beat of the first draft.)

**[01]** [ON SCREEN: FBI surveillance print: Duquesne in the Sawyer office, slow push toward the two men.]

Behind a mirror in a small office near Times Square, a camera is running. On the other side of the glass, two men sit at a desk.

**[02]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) FBI surveillance print: Duquesne in the Sawyer office, slow push toward the two men.]

One is a German-born engineer who calls himself Harry Sawyer. The other is sixty-three years old, and he has been a spy for most of his life.

**[03]** [ON SCREEN: Close crop on Duquesne's face in the same print.]

His name is Frederick Duquesne. He has fought the British in South Africa, escaped from their prison camps, and worked for German intelligence in two world wars.

**[04]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Close crop on Duquesne's face in the same print.]

He has used about thirty names. He trusts almost nobody. And he is talking, on film, to the Federal Bureau of Investigation.

**[05]** [ON SCREEN: Mug-shot sheet of the thirty-three, slow pan down the rows.]

Within days of this picture, thirty-three people would be in custody. All of them would be convicted. The FBI still calls it the largest espionage case in American history to end in convictions. Their sentences added up to more than three hundred years.

**[06]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'THE NORDEN BOMBSIGHT — JANUARY 9, 1938 — LEFT NEW YORK IN AN UMBRELLA'.]

But there is a detail in the file that the famous version of this story tends to skip. The most valuable American secret this network ever stole had already left New York, hidden inside an umbrella, two years before the FBI's case even began. Hold on to that.

**[07]** [ON SCREEN: Card: the question.]

So this is not really the story of a secret saved. It is the story of something stranger.

**[08]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: the question.]

How the FBI, for the first time, took over an enemy spy service's own line of communication, and ran it for sixteen months. And how the man who made that possible was almost erased from the story.

**[09]** [ON SCREEN: Title card: THE DEFECTOR FILES — Case file: The Duquesne Spy Ring, 1941.]

This is The Defector Files.

**[10]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card I · The man from Mülheim. Archive scene: New York harbor / liner.]

The file begins with a man named Gottlieb Adolf Wilhelm Sebold. He was born in Mülheim, in the Rhineland, on March tenth, 1899. He served in the German army's engineers during the First World War.

**[11]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Chapter card I · The man from Mülheim. Archive scene: New York harbor / liner.]

In 1922 he emigrated to the United States, and over the next years he worked in industrial plants and aircraft factories, in America and in South America.

**[12]** [ON SCREEN: Map: Mülheim, Hamburg, Cologne, Genoa, New York.]

On February tenth, 1936, he became an American citizen. He went by William. He had a wife, Helen, and a life in the United States.

**[13]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Map: Mülheim, Hamburg, Cologne, Genoa, New York.]

In February 1939 he sailed back to Germany to visit his mother in Mülheim. It was meant to be a family visit. It did not stay one.

**[14]** [ON SCREEN: Hamburg harbor archive image.]

When his ship docked in Hamburg, a man from the Gestapo approached him. According to the FBI's account, the man told Sebold that he would be contacted, because of what he knew about American aircraft plants.

**[15]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Hamburg harbor archive image.]

Then he let him go. Sebold traveled on to Mülheim and found work there.

**[16]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'SEPTEMBER 1939 — DR. GASSNER · DR. RENKEN'.]

In September 1939, the month the war began in Europe, a visitor came to see him. He called himself Doctor Gassner.

**[17]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: 'SEPTEMBER 1939 — DR. GASSNER · DR. RENKEN'.]

He asked detailed questions about military aircraft and equipment in the United States. Then he made an offer. Sebold should go back to America and work for German intelligence.

**[18]** [ON SCREEN: Document card: naturalization form, highlight on 'Have you ever been arrested?']

When Sebold hesitated, Gassner and a second man, who called himself Doctor Renken, used leverage. Years earlier Sebold had served time in a German jail, and he had left that off his American naturalization papers.

**[19]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Document card: naturalization form, highlight on 'Have you ever been arrested?']

If he refused, they would make sure the Americans found out. There were threats against his family too.

**[20]** [ON SCREEN: Archive portrait of Nikolaus Ritter if available; otherwise card 'DR. RENKEN = MAJOR NIKOLAUS RITTER, ABWEHR'.]

Doctor Renken was not a doctor. He was Major Nikolaus Ritter of the Abwehr, the German military intelligence service, head of its air intelligence work out of Hamburg.

**[21]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive portrait of Nikolaus Ritter if available; otherwise card 'DR. RENKEN = MAJOR NIKOLAUS RITTER, ABWEHR'.]

Ritter had lived in New York for years in the nineteen twenties. He knew America, and he had already run agents there.

**[22]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'HARRY SAWYER · CODE NAME TRAMP · ABWEHR NO. A.3549'.]

Sebold agreed. He was sent to a training school in Hamburg for about seven weeks. He learned to work a clandestine shortwave radio, to use codes, and to read microphotographs, messages shrunk to the size of a dot.

**[23]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: 'HARRY SAWYER · CODE NAME TRAMP · ABWEHR NO. A.3549'.]

He was given a cover name, Harry Sawyer, a code name, TRAMP, and an Abwehr number, A three five four nine.

**[24]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: Cologne, cathedral/city scene.]

And then, before he left Germany, he did something the Abwehr never found out about. He went to the American consulate in Cologne, and he insisted on seeing the Consul General himself.

**[25]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive image: Cologne, cathedral/city scene.]

He said he had been blackmailed. He said he was a loyal American citizen. And he said he wanted to work with the FBI.

**[26]** [ON SCREEN: Card: analysis — 'WHY DID IT WORK?']

Here the file invites a question, and this is interpretation rather than fact. Why did the Abwehr trust a man it had recruited by blackmail? Coercion can force someone to say yes. It cannot make them loyal.

**[27]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: analysis — 'WHY DID IT WORK?']

Ritter had built his operation on a recruit with every reason to hate him, and then sent that recruit to a country where he had a home, a wife and a citizenship to protect.

**[28]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card II · The office near Times Square. Archive scene: Times Square / Manhattan 1940.]

Sebold sailed from Genoa and reached New York on February eighth, 1940. The FBI was waiting.

**[29]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Chapter card II · The office near Times Square. Archive scene: Times Square / Manhattan 1940.]

Over the following weeks, agents and Sebold built a cover for Harry Sawyer: a consulting diesel engineer, with his own firm, the Diesel Research Company, and an office in the Times Square district of Manhattan.

**[30]** [ON SCREEN: Diagram card: office plan — desk, two-way mirror, hidden microphones, camera room next door.]

The office was a stage. There were hidden microphones. One wall held a mirror that was really a window, and in the room behind it FBI agents could watch, listen and film. Everything a German agent said at Sawyer's desk could be shown to a jury.

**[31]** [ON SCREEN: Hoover portrait, April 1940 (Harris & Ewing).]

The case went to the top of the Bureau. Director J. Edgar Hoover followed it closely, and briefed President Franklin Roosevelt on it.

**[32]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Hoover portrait, April 1940 (Harris & Ewing).]

Special Agent James Ellsworth was assigned to stay with Sebold for the entire investigation. For sixteen months, he would shadow almost every move the double agent made.

**[33]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: shortwave radio / radio equipment.]

The second part of the plan was the radio. Ritter had told Sebold to set up a shortwave station and contact Hamburg.

**[34]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive image: shortwave radio / radio equipment.]

So in May 1940, on Long Island, FBI laboratory engineers built one. The men who tapped out the messages were FBI agents. To the Abwehr, it was TRAMP's transmitter in America.

**[35]** [ON SCREEN: Card: '300+ MESSAGES SENT · ~200 RECEIVED · 16 MONTHS'.]

Over the next sixteen months, that station became one of the main channels between German agents in New York and their headquarters.

**[36]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: '300+ MESSAGES SENT · ~200 RECEIVED · 16 MONTHS'.]

The FBI says it sent more than three hundred messages to Germany and received about two hundred. Everything going out was checked first. Some of it was harmless. Some of it was false.

**[37]** [ON SCREEN: Card: analysis — 'A CHANNEL, NOT A SPY'.]

This is the part of the case that matters most, and it is easy to miss. The FBI was not simply watching spies. It had become the spies' post office.

**[38]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: analysis — 'A CHANNEL, NOT A SPY'.]

Every German agent who wanted a report to reach Hamburg quickly had a reason to bring it to Sawyer. The trap did not need to chase anyone. It only needed to wait.

**[39]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card III · DUNN. Duquesne portrait 1916 (Bain).]

One of the first names on Sebold's list was a code name: DUNN. Ritter wanted him to find DUNN in New York. DUNN was Frederick Joubert Duquesne. He was born in the Cape Colony, in South Africa, on September twenty-first, 1877, to a Boer family.

**[40]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: Boer War scene / Boer commandos.]

His own account of his life reads like an adventure serial. He fought for the Boers against the British. He said he was captured by the British three times and by the Portuguese once, and escaped every time.

**[41]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive image: Boer War scene / Boer commandos.]

He was shipped as a prisoner of war to Bermuda, and got away to the United States, where he became a citizen in 1913.

**[42]** [ON SCREEN: Duquesne 1916 portrait, close.]

In New York he wrote for the New York Herald, lectured on big game hunting, and claimed to have taught Theodore Roosevelt to shoot.

**[43]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Duquesne 1916 portrait, close.]

In 1910 he was even part of a serious proposal to bring hippopotamuses into the Louisiana bayous, to ease an American meat shortage. That one, remarkably, is documented.

**[44]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: HMS Hampshire or Kitchener portrait.]

Other parts are not. His most famous boast was that in 1916 he had sunk the British cruiser Hampshire with Lord Kitchener aboard. A 1932 biography was even titled The Man Who Killed Kitchener.

**[45]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive image: HMS Hampshire or Kitchener portrait.]

The record points elsewhere. The Hampshire struck a mine laid by a German submarine. There is no good evidence that Duquesne was anywhere near it.

**[46]** [ON SCREEN: Card: analysis — 'THE LEGEND VERSUS THE FILE'.]

So the file has to separate two men. The legend, which Duquesne wrote himself and told to anyone who would listen. And the record, which is smaller but still real.

**[47]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: analysis — 'THE LEGEND VERSUS THE FILE'.]

In the First World War he did work for Germany, against British shipping in South America. In 1917 he was arrested in New York, faked paralysis in a hospital prison ward for nearly two years, and escaped.

**[48]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: New York street / automat.]

By 1937 he was back in touch with Ritter. When Sebold finally met him, Duquesne was immediately suspicious of the office. He passed Sebold a note suggesting they should talk somewhere else. The two men went to an automat, a coin-operated cafeteria, and talked there instead.

**[49]** [ON SCREEN: Card: Newkirk quote about Duquesne's counter-surveillance.]

The agents assigned to Duquesne found him hard work. Agent Raymond Newkirk rented the room above his apartment near Central Park and listened through a hidden microphone. Newkirk later described how Duquesne moved through the city.

**[50]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: Newkirk quote about Duquesne's counter-surveillance.]

He would take a local train, change to an express, change back to a local, go through a revolving door and keep going right around. Duquesne even confronted one of the agents following him.

**[51]** [ON SCREEN: FBI surveillance print, wider crop on the desk and papers.]

And yet he kept coming back to Sawyer's office. That is the paradox at the center of this case. The most careful man in the network used the one channel that was not his.

**[52]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) FBI surveillance print, wider crop on the desk and papers.]

He brought photographs and specifications of a new bomb. He explained how fires could be started in industrial plants. He said he had slipped into a DuPont plant in Delaware. That is his claim, not a fact in the file.

**[53]** [ON SCREEN: Card: quoted letter — 'Don't be concerned if this information is confidential...']

Much of what he gathered came not from break-ins but from the mail. He wrote to companies and government offices as a writer and lecturer, and asked.

**[54]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: quoted letter — 'Don't be concerned if this information is confidential...']

One letter went to the Army's Chemical Warfare Service, about a new gas mask. He told them not to be concerned if the information was confidential, because it would be in the hands of a good, patriotic citizen.

**[55]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card IV · The network. Mug-shot sheet, crop on top rows.]

Through the office and the radio, the rest of the network came into view. It was not a ring of professional officers.

**[56]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Chapter card IV · The network. Mug-shot sheet, crop on top rows.]

It was mostly ordinary people with ordinary jobs, placed where secrets passed by. Ship's cooks, butchers, bakers and stewards who could carry letters across the Atlantic. A truck driver. A carpenter. A musician.

**[57]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: Pan American clipper flying boat.]

Rene Mezenen was a steward on Pan American's transatlantic clipper. He could carry papers from New York to Lisbon in about a day, and he reported the convoys he saw from the air.

**[58]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive image: Pan American clipper flying boat.]

He boasted to Sebold that he hid letters so well that finding them would mean taking the airplane apart.

**[59]** [ON SCREEN: Mug-shot sheet crop on Roeder / archive image: Sperry gyroscope.]

Everett Roeder was born in the Bronx. He was an engineer at the Sperry Gyroscope Company, working on confidential designs for the Army and Navy.

**[60]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Mug-shot sheet crop on Roeder / archive image: Sperry gyroscope.]

According to the FBI, he passed on blueprints for the radio equipment of a new Glenn Martin bomber, range finders and blind-flying instruments. When agents searched his Long Island home, they found sixteen guns.

**[61]** [ON SCREEN: Mug-shot sheet crop on Lilly Stein.]

Lilly Stein had come from Vienna. She had trained at the same Hamburg school as Sebold, and in New York she moved in social circles and gathered what she could. Her address was used by others as a return address for material bound for Germany.

**[62]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'ROBIN MOOR — SUNK MAY 21, 1941'. Map of the South Atlantic.]

And the stakes were not abstract. Prosecutors later told the jury that Leo Waalen, a painter at a boatyard building small craft for the Navy, had handed in the sailing date of an American freighter, the Robin Moor, for transmission to Germany.

**[63]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: 'ROBIN MOOR — SUNK MAY 21, 1941'. Map of the South Atlantic.]

In May 1941 a U-boat sank the Robin Moor in the Atlantic off West Africa. Everyone aboard survived, after days in lifeboats. The file does not show that his report caused the sinking.

**[64]** [ON SCREEN: Card: Strunck episode.]

Some ideas were stopped in the office itself. A seaman named Erich Strunck asked for permission to steal the diplomatic bag of a British officer traveling on his ship, and to push the officer overboard.

**[65]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: Strunck episode.]

Sebold talked him out of it, telling him it was too risky. Another member, Paul Bante, brought dynamite and detonators to the office.

**[66]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card V · The umbrella. Card: Norden bombsight text.]

Now, the umbrella. Hermann Lang was an inspector at the Carl L. Norden company, which made the Norden bombsight, one of the most closely guarded instruments in the American military. In 1937 Lang met Ritter in New York and told him he had access to the drawings.

**[67]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'KITCHEN TABLE · NIGHT · 1937'; archive scene of New York pier and liner.]

According to the FBI, Lang took the drawings home and copied them at his kitchen table at night, while his family slept.

**[68]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: 'KITCHEN TABLE · NIGHT · 1937'; archive scene of New York pier and liner.]

On January ninth, 1938, he handed the copies, hidden in the wooden casing of an umbrella, to a courier aboard a ship bound for Bremen. He was paid fifteen hundred dollars, and later invited to Germany to help rebuild the device.

**[69]** [ON SCREEN: Card: analysis — 'WHAT DID THE THEFT ACHIEVE?']

This is where historians are careful, and so is this file. How much did Germany gain?

**[70]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: analysis — 'WHAT DID THE THEFT ACHIEVE?']

German bombsights of the war used a stabilized, gyroscopic design with similarities to the Norden, but the Luftwaffe also developed its own instruments, and there is no agreement that Lang's drawings changed the air war.

**[71]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: analysis — 'WHAT DID THE THEFT ACHIEVE?']

What is clear is the timing. The theft happened in 1937 and 1938, long before Sebold's first message.

**[72]** [ON SCREEN: Mug-shot sheet crop on Lang.]

So the case did not prevent the ring's greatest coup. What it did was find the man behind it. Ritter, in Germany, still wanted Lang's help, and Sebold was told to contact him.

**[73]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Mug-shot sheet crop on Lang.]

Through the office, the FBI connected the bombsight theft to a living network in New York. Lang refused an offer to be taken to safety in Germany. He stayed at home in Queens.

**[74]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card VI · The roundup. FBI surveillance print dated June 25, 1941.]

By the early summer of 1941, the Bureau had what it needed: films, recordings, intercepted letters and sixteen months of radio traffic.

**[75]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Chapter card VI · The roundup. FBI surveillance print dated June 25, 1941.]

The FBI print of Duquesne in Sawyer's office is dated June twenty-fifth, 1941. A few days later, at the end of June, agents moved in on the whole network.

**[76]** [ON SCREEN: Mug-shot sheet, full.]

Thirty-three people were arrested and charged. Nineteen pleaded guilty. A Japanese naval officer who had met Sebold, and been filmed doing it, slipped away to the West Coast and sailed for Tokyo. He was never tried in America.

**[77]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: Brooklyn federal court / courtroom scene; card 'SEPTEMBER 3, 1941 — BROOKLYN'.]

The other fourteen went on trial in Federal District Court in Brooklyn on September third, 1941. The jury watched the FBI's films from behind the mirror. They saw Duquesne at Sawyer's desk. They heard the men from the ships and the factories talking about convoys, bombs and plants.

**[78]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'DECEMBER 13, 1941 — ALL FOURTEEN GUILTY'.]

The trial ran through the autumn. On December seventh, 1941, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, and within days the United States was at war with Germany too. On December thirteenth, the jury returned its verdicts. All fourteen were guilty.

**[79]** [ON SCREEN: Card: sentences list — Duquesne 18 yrs, Lang 18, Roeder 16, Stein 10, total 300+ years.]

On January second, 1942, the sentences came down. Duquesne received eighteen years for espionage. So did Lang. Roeder was given sixteen. Lilly Stein ten.

**[80]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: sentences list — Duquesne 18 yrs, Lang 18, Roeder 16, Stein 10, total 300+ years.]

Together, more than three hundred years in prison. A German intelligence officer would later call the roundup the death blow to German espionage in the United States.

**[81]** [ON SCREEN: Hoover 1940 informal photo.]

Hoover called it the greatest spy roundup in American history, and the Bureau told the story often. Four years later Hollywood told it too, as The House on Ninety-Second Street, with the names changed.

**[82]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Hoover 1940 informal photo.]

The FBI's version made the Bureau the hero. Hold on to that as well, because it shaped who was remembered.

**[83]** [ON SCREEN: Card: link to Operation Pastorius — 'JUNE 1942: SABOTEURS BY U-BOAT'.]

There is a consequence that connects this case to another file. With its networks in America gone, the Abwehr could no longer rely on agents already in place.

**[84]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: link to Operation Pastorius — 'JUNE 1942: SABOTEURS BY U-BOAT'.]

In June 1942, it sent saboteurs instead, landed by U-boat on Long Island and in Florida. That operation, Pastorius, is a story of its own. But it began with the empty space this case left behind.

**[85]** [ON SCREEN: Chapter card VII · After. Archive image: Leavenworth penitentiary.]

Duquesne served his sentence at Leavenworth, in Kansas. According to the accounts of his life, he was mistreated and beaten by other inmates.

**[86]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Chapter card VII · After. Archive image: Leavenworth penitentiary.]

In 1954, in poor health, he was released, after about fourteen years. He died penniless at City Hospital on Welfare Island in New York, on May twenty-fourth, 1956. He was seventy-eight.

**[87]** [ON SCREEN: Archive image: California farm / landscape.]

Sebold disappeared. He was given a new identity and moved to California, where he tried, among other things, to run a chicken farm.

**[88]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Archive image: California farm / landscape.]

He struggled to hold a job and was often ill. Letters from Germany suggested that people there still wanted revenge, and he lived with that fear.

**[89]** [ON SCREEN: Card: 'NAPA STATE HOSPITAL, 1965–1970'.]

In 1965 he was committed to Napa State Hospital, diagnosed with manic depression. He died there on February sixteenth, 1970, at the age of seventy.

**[90]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: 'NAPA STATE HOSPITAL, 1965–1970'.]

A book had told his story in 1943. It was not until 2014 that a full history, by the journalist Peter Duffy, called him the first hero of the Second World War.

**[91]** [ON SCREEN: Card: analysis — 'WHO GETS THE CREDIT?']

Here is the final judgment of this file, and it is an interpretation. The famous version of this case is about the FBI's skill, and the skill was real.

**[92]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: analysis — 'WHO GETS THE CREDIT?']

But the decisive act came first, and it came from one frightened man in Cologne who walked into a consulate when he could have stayed silent. Without that visit, there is no office, no radio and no trial.

**[93]** [ON SCREEN: Card: what the case changed.]

What the case changed was method. It showed that the best way to fight a spy service was not only to arrest its agents, but to become its channel: to control what it heard and to let it lead you to everyone else.

**[94]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) Card: what the case changed.]

In Britain, MI5 was running the same idea at the same time, and on a far larger scale, turning captured German agents into what became the Double Cross system.

**[95]** [ON SCREEN: (cont.) (cont.) Card: what the case changed.]

One of its first double agents, a Welshman code-named SNOW, had also been recruited by Major Ritter. The same spymaster was beaten twice, on both sides of the Atlantic, by the same method.

**[96]** [ON SCREEN: Mug-shot sheet, slow pull out.]

Thirty-three people were convicted. Much of what they handed over after Sebold's arrival went through a channel the FBI controlled. And the one secret that mattered most had escaped before anyone was watching, inside an umbrella.

**[97]** [ON SCREEN: Card: sign-off.]

This file is closed. New files are declassified every day on The Defector Files.
