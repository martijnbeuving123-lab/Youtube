# The Brides in the Bath case — script

Working title: **The Bathtub Test That Exposed A Killer: The Brides In The Bath (1915)**
Format: one video, about 20 minutes. Narrator: Daniel (vidIQ). British spelling.
Each beat = one visual block. `[ON SCREEN: …]` is the image or graphic for that block; the
lines under it are the narration exactly as voiced. Reconstructions and diagrams are labelled
as such on screen; no image is presented as evidence unless it is archive material.

## COLD OPEN

### 01
[ON SCREEN: black. Title text: Kentish Town police station, London, 1915. An empty zinc bath, slow push-in (card).]

Kentish Town police station, north London, in the early months of 1915. In a back room stands an ordinary household bath, filled with water. A young woman in a bathing costume is lying in it. A Scotland Yard detective is standing at the end of the tub.

### 02
[ON SCREEN: card: "Volunteer, similar height and build" over a dark background.]

She is a volunteer, chosen because she is about the same height and build as a woman who died in a bath two and a half years earlier, at the seaside, in Kent. The detective has already tried to push her under the water by force. Each time, she struggled, splashed and grabbed the sides. It was obvious, and it was noisy.

### 03
[ON SCREEN: slow zoom on archive portrait of Arthur Neil if available, else card "Divisional Detective Inspector Arthur Neil".]

Then, according to his own later account, he does something different. Without warning, he takes hold of her feet and lifts them. Her head slides under the surface. And she stops moving. It reportedly took the detective and a doctor around half an hour to bring her round.

### 04
[ON SCREEN: three cards side by side: Herne Bay 1912, Blackpool 1913, Highgate 1914.]

Three women had died in baths like this one, in three different towns, over two and a half years. Each death had been examined by a doctor. Each had gone before a coroner. And each had been ruled an accident.

### 05
[ON SCREEN: archive photograph of George Joseph Smith, slow push-in.]

All three had married the same man, under three different names. And within months, that experiment in a police station would help put something in front of a British jury that it was rarely allowed to hear: not one death, but a pattern.

### 06
[ON SCREEN: title card — PRECINCT ARCHIVE. Case file: The Brides in the Bath, 1915.]

This is Precinct Archive. This is the case of the Brides in the Bath, and how three bathtubs, a newspaper cutting and a pathologist's tape measure became a turning point in British forensic science.

## ACT I — THE MAN WITH MANY NAMES

### 07
[ON SCREEN: archive portrait of Smith, profile. Caption: George Joseph Smith, born 1872.]

George Joseph Smith was born in Bethnal Green, in the East End of London, on the 11th of January, 1872. His father was an insurance agent. By the age of nine, the boy had been sent to a reformatory at Gravesend, in Kent. As a young man he was in and out of prison for theft and fraud.

### 08
[ON SCREEN: card: "1896 — twelve months for persuading a woman to steal from her employers".]

In 1896, he was sentenced to twelve months for persuading a woman to steal from her employers. It is the first appearance in the file of something that would matter later. Smith did not rob strangers. He worked through women who trusted him.

### 09
[ON SCREEN: map of England: Leicester, London, Worthing.]

With the proceeds he opened a baker's shop in Leicester. In 1898, calling himself George Oliver Love, he married Caroline Thornhill. It was the only legal marriage he would ever make. The couple moved to London, where she took jobs as a maid and, according to the record, stole from her employers for him.

### 10
[ON SCREEN: card: "1901 — two years".]

She was caught in Worthing and served a year. When she came out, she gave evidence against her husband, and in January 1901 he was sent to prison for two years. When he was released, Caroline left England for Canada, and out of his reach.

### 11
[ON SCREEN: card: a column of names appearing one by one: George Oliver Love, George Rose Smith, Henry Williams, Charles Oliver James, John Lloyd.]

Over the next six years, Smith would marry again and again, never once divorced. Between 1908 and 1914, he went through seven bigamous marriages, under a string of names. George Rose Smith. Henry Williams. Charles Oliver James. John Lloyd.

### 12
[ON SCREEN: map: Worthing, Camden Town, Bristol.]

The method rarely changed. In June 1908 he married Florence Wilson, a widow from Worthing. Within a few weeks he was gone, along with about thirty pounds from her savings and whatever of her belongings he could sell from their rooms in Camden Town.

### 13
[ON SCREEN: card: "Edith Pegler, Bristol, 1908".]

That same summer, in Bristol, he married Edith Pegler, who had answered his advertisement for a housekeeper. Edith was different. She was the one he kept coming back to. He told her he dealt in antiques and had to travel. He would disappear for months, and return with money.

### 14
[ON SCREEN: card: "Sarah Freeman, 1909 — accounts put the loss at about £400".]

In October 1909, as George Rose Smith, he married Sarah Freeman. He emptied her savings and sold her bonds. Accounts put the total at about four hundred pounds. Then he vanished. Up to this point, the file describes a bigamist and a thief. Nothing in it yet describes a killer.

### 15
[ON SCREEN: map: Weymouth, Dorset. Caption: Weymouth, 1910.]

In 1910, in the seaside town of Weymouth, in Dorset, a man calling himself Henry Williams met a woman named Beatrice Mundy. Everyone called her Bessie. She was in her early thirties, and, unlike his earlier wives, she had real money.

### 16
[ON SCREEN: archive photograph: George Joseph Smith and Bessie Mundy. Caption: Bessie Mundy and 'Henry Williams'.]

Her father, reportedly a bank manager, had left her a sum that accounts put at about two and a half thousand pounds. But it was held in trust by her family, and paid out to her as a small allowance. She could not simply hand it over. They married in Weymouth that summer.

### 17
[ON SCREEN: card: "The money was in trust".]

When it became clear that the capital could not be reached, Williams left her, taking what cash he could. Hold on to that detail. The money in trust was the one obstacle he could not get round while Bessie was alive. There was one way round it, and it needed a will.

### 18
[ON SCREEN: map: Weston-super-Mare. Caption: Weston-super-Mare, 1912.]

For almost two years, Bessie heard nothing from him. Then, in 1912, she was staying in Weston-super-Mare when, by chance, she met him on the seafront. Whatever he said to her that day, it worked. She took him back.

### 19
[ON SCREEN: map: Herne Bay, Kent. Caption: 80 High Street, Herne Bay.]

By the early summer they had taken a house at 80 High Street, in Herne Bay, a small resort on the north Kent coast. The house had no bathroom. Accounts say that Williams went to a local ironmonger and bought a bath, haggling over the price.

### 20
[ON SCREEN: card: "July 1912 — two wills".]

In early July 1912, Bessie and her husband made wills, each in favour of the other. A few days later, Williams took his wife to a local doctor, Frank French. She complained of headaches. It was her husband who described fits, and the doctor who wrote down a possible case of epilepsy.

### 21
[ON SCREEN: slow push on a dark Herne Bay seafront image or card "Friday, 12 July 1912".]

On the night of Friday, the 12th of July, Williams went to fetch Dr French, saying his wife had had another seizure. French saw her, and said he would call again. The next morning, Saturday the 13th, he was told that Bessie Williams was dead.

### 22
[ON SCREEN: card: "Inquest — death by misadventure".]

French found her in the bath, her head under the water, her legs stretched out straight, her feet resting above the surface at the far end. There was no sign of violence. He put the drowning down to an epileptic fit. The inquest agreed. Her estate, the money that had been held in trust, passed to her husband.

### 23
[ON SCREEN: slow zoom out on the Smith–Mundy photograph.]

That position in the bath would turn out to be one of the most important facts in the whole case. Nobody saw it that way in 1912. Henry Williams disappeared from Herne Bay, and went back to Edith Pegler in Bristol, with money.

## ACT II — TWO NEWSPAPER CUTTINGS

### 24
[ON SCREEN: map: Southsea and Portsmouth. Caption: II · Blackpool and Highgate.]

Late in 1913, now using his own name, George Smith married Alice Burnham, a young nurse from Buckinghamshire, at a register office in Portsmouth. Her father, Charles Burnham, did not take to his new son-in-law. Hold on to that, too, because Charles Burnham would not let go.

### 25
[ON SCREEN: card: "Life policy on Alice Burnham — about £500".]

Shortly after the wedding, a life insurance policy was taken out on Alice, worth about five hundred pounds, with her husband to benefit. In December the couple went north, to Blackpool, for what was described as a short holiday.

### 26
[ON SCREEN: archive illustration from 1915 news coverage of Alice Burnham (Commons), slow pan. Caption: Alice Burnham.]

Accounts say the first lodgings they looked at were turned down because there was no bath. They took rooms instead at a boarding house in Regent Road, kept by Joseph and Alice Crossley. One account of the case says Smith looked at the bath before they agreed to stay.

### 27
[ON SCREEN: card: "Friday, 12 December 1913. Blackpool."]

On the evening of Friday, the 12th of December, 1913, Alice took a bath. It was her husband who found her. A doctor was called. The inquest found that she had drowned by accident, perhaps after fainting in the hot water. A newspaper in Blackpool reported it. Smith, reportedly, arranged the cheapest burial on offer, and left.

### 28
[ON SCREEN: map: Bath and Highgate. Caption: Bath, 17 December 1914.]

A year passed. In December 1914, in the city of Bath, a man calling himself John Lloyd married Margaret Elizabeth Lofty. She was thirty-eight, and, accounts say, a clergyman's daughter. Three days before the wedding, she had taken out a life policy worth about seven hundred pounds.

### 29
[ON SCREEN: card: "14 Bismarck Road, Highgate".]

Straight after the wedding, the couple took lodgings at 14 Bismarck Road, in Highgate, north London. On the 18th of December, Margaret went to a solicitor and made a new will, leaving everything to her husband. She also withdrew her savings. It was a little over three hours before she died.

### 30
[ON SCREEN: slow push on dark card: "Friday, 18 December 1914".]

That evening she took a bath. According to the way the landlady's evidence is usually told, she heard splashing from the bathroom, then quiet, and then the sound of the harmonium in the sitting room. Shortly afterwards, Mr Lloyd went out, came back, and called for help. His wife was found dead in the bath.

### 31
[ON SCREEN: card: "Inquest — accidental death".]

The coroner's doctor found almost nothing: no sign of a struggle, only a tiny bruise above the left elbow. The verdict was accidental death. It was the third. And this time, a newspaper carried the story a long way beyond north London.

### 32
[ON SCREEN: newspaper-style card: "News of the World, December 1914" (reconstruction, labelled).]

Shortly before Christmas, the News of the World printed a short report of the death of Mrs Margaret Lloyd, found in her bath at her lodgings in Highgate. In Buckinghamshire, Charles Burnham read it. So, in Blackpool, did Joseph Crossley. Both were struck by the same thing.

### 33
[ON SCREEN: card (letter style): "3 January 1915 — Blackpool — to the Metropolitan Police".]

On the 3rd of January, 1915, Crossley wrote to the Metropolitan Police, on behalf of himself, his wife and Alice's father. He enclosed two cuttings: the report of Margaret Lloyd's death, and the report of the Blackpool inquest into the death of Alice Smith a year earlier. The two deaths, he wrote, were strikingly similar.

### 34
[ON SCREEN: archive photo (if available) or card: "Divisional Detective Inspector Arthur Neil, Kentish Town".]

The letter reached Divisional Detective Inspector Arthur Neil, at Kentish Town. It was wartime. Neil's division was already busy with enemy aliens and the other duties of a country at war. A link between two accidental drownings, more than two hundred miles apart, could easily have been filed and forgotten.

### 35
[ON SCREEN: card: "The tub was three-quarters full".]

Instead, Neil went to Bismarck Road and looked at the bath. He found it hard to believe that a grown woman could have drowned by accident in a tub that small, a tub that had been three-quarters full. That is an inference, not a fact from the file, but it is the inference that started the inquiry.

### 36
[ON SCREEN: card: "Yorkshire Insurance Company, 12 January 1915".]

Then the file gave him something firmer. On the 12th of January, the coroner's doctor told Neil that he had received an enquiry from the Yorkshire Insurance Company. Mr Lloyd was claiming on his wife's policy. Neil asked the doctor to hold his reply, and requested everything Blackpool had on the death of Alice Smith.

### 37
[ON SCREEN: card listing parallels: new marriage · insurance or will · bath checked in advance · found by husband · no marks.]

Blackpool's answer made the pattern sharper. Alice, too, had been newly married. Alice, too, had been insured, and had made a will in her husband's favour. Both men had found their wives in the bath. Both had left soon afterwards. And from the descriptions, Mr Smith of Blackpool and Mr Lloyd of Highgate sounded very alike.

### 38
[ON SCREEN: card: "The solicitor's office was watched day and night".]

Neil now set a trap. He asked the doctor to send the insurers a favourable report, so that the claim would go ahead. He was counting on Mr Lloyd to call at his solicitor's office to collect, and he had the office watched, day and night. Would the man come himself?

## ACT III — THE BATHTUBS

### 39
[ON SCREEN: card: "1 February 1915". Caption: III · Three baths in Kentish Town.]

On the 1st of February, 1915, a man matching the description arrived. Neil stepped forward and asked if he was John Lloyd. He said he was. Neil then asked whether he was also George Smith. The man denied it, firmly. Neil told him he was being held on suspicion of bigamy. Before long, he admitted that he was indeed George Smith.

### 40
[ON SCREEN: card: "Charge: bigamy. Suspicion: murder."]

Bigamy was a charge Neil could prove. Murder was another matter. Two coroners had already ruled the deaths accidental. There were no witnesses, no weapon, no marks worth the name. If the case was going to be made, it would have to be made from the bodies, and from the baths.

### 41
[ON SCREEN: archive portrait of Bernard Spilsbury if available, else card "Bernard Spilsbury, Home Office pathologist".]

The Home Office turned to Bernard Spilsbury. He was thirty-seven, and already known from the Crippen trial five years earlier, where his evidence about a scar had helped convict a man of murder. Margaret Lloyd's body was exhumed.

### 42
[ON SCREEN: card: "Findings: a small bruise. Two tiny marks. Few signs of drowning."]

Spilsbury confirmed the small bruise above the elbow, and found two other tiny marks. He found no heart disease and no illness that might explain a sudden collapse. Even the signs of drowning were slight. Everything suggested that death had come almost at once. He had the organs tested for poison. There was none.

### 43
[ON SCREEN: map: Herne Bay highlighted. Caption: 8 February 1915.]

By now the newspapers were running stories about the brides in the bath, and on the 8th of February a report arrived from the senior police officer in Herne Bay. It described the death of a Mrs Bessie Williams, in a bath, in July 1912. Neil sent photographs of his prisoner to Kent. The answer came back: Henry Williams was George Smith.

### 44
[ON SCREEN: card: three names joined by lines — Henry Williams = George Smith = John Lloyd.]

Three women. Three names for the husband. Three verdicts of accident. Spilsbury went north to Blackpool to examine Alice Smith, and found the same picture as in Highgate: no violence, sudden death, very little sign of drowning.

### 45
[ON SCREEN: card: "Bessie Mundy — goose flesh on the thigh".]

Then he examined Bessie Mundy. Here, after more than two years, he found one clear sign that she had drowned: goose flesh on the skin of her thigh, a sign associated with a sudden plunge into water. The question was no longer whether she had drowned. It was how.

### 46
[ON SCREEN: illustration (labelled reconstruction): diagram of a 5-foot bath with a 5 ft 7 in figure, sloping head end.]

The three baths were brought to London. Spilsbury measured them, and measured the women. Bessie had been five feet seven inches tall. The bath in Herne Bay was about five feet long. And he thought hard about what an epileptic fit actually does to the body.

### 47
[ON SCREEN: diagram continued: arrow showing body stiffening and sliding up the sloping end.]

In the first stage of a fit, he reasoned, the whole body goes rigid and stretches out. In a short bath with a sloping end, that would push a tall woman's head and shoulders up the slope, out of the water, not under it. In the next stage, the limbs jerk. Nothing in that sequence would slide her head beneath the surface.

### 48
[ON SCREEN: diagram: feet raised at the narrow end, head sliding down under the water line.]

Now recall where Dr French had found her: her head under the water, her legs out straight, her feet raised at the narrow end. Spilsbury's conclusion was that this position was very hard to reach by accident. But if someone at the foot of the bath suddenly lifted her feet, her head and body would slide under at once.

### 49
[ON SCREEN: card: "Water rushing into the nose and throat — sudden loss of consciousness".]

The sudden rush of water into the nose and throat, he suggested, could cause shock and almost immediate loss of consciousness. No struggle. No bruises. Few of the usual signs of drowning. It was a theory that explained the very absence of evidence that had made all three deaths look innocent.

### 50
[ON SCREEN: return to the opening image of the zinc bath in the police station (card).]

It still had to be tested. This brings the file back to that room in Kentish Town. Neil's volunteers, of similar build to the dead women, struggled visibly when he tried to force them under. When he lifted the feet, one of them went under without a fight, and lost consciousness. It is Neil's own account, and it is reported, not independently recorded. But it matched what Spilsbury had predicted.

### 51
[ON SCREEN: card: "23 March 1915 — charged with three murders".]

On the 23rd of March, 1915, George Joseph Smith was formally charged with the murders of Bessie Mundy, Alice Burnham and Margaret Lofty. But under English law he could be tried for only one of them at a time. The prosecution chose Bessie Mundy, the first. And that created the real problem. Would the jury be allowed to hear about the other two?

## ACT IV — THE TRIAL AND THE SYSTEM

### 52
[ON SCREEN: archive image of the Old Bailey (Central Criminal Court) if available, else card. Caption: IV · The Old Bailey, 22 June 1915.]

The trial opened at the Old Bailey on the 22nd of June, 1915, before Mr Justice Scrutton. For the Crown, Archibald Bodkin, later Director of Public Prosecutions, with Travers Humphreys and Cecil Whiteley. For the defence, the most famous advocate of the day, Sir Edward Marshall Hall.

### 53
[ON SCREEN: card: "Similar-fact evidence — 'system'".]

Marshall Hall's first battle was over the other two deaths. A man on trial for one killing, he argued, should be judged on the evidence of that killing, not on the suspicion of others. If the jury heard about Blackpool and Highgate, the verdict on Herne Bay would be decided before a word of it was examined.

### 54
[ON SCREEN: card (quote style): "evidence of system" — the judge's ruling.]

The judge disagreed. The deaths in Blackpool and Highgate, he ruled, could be heard, not to prove that Smith had killed those women, but to show a system: to answer the claim that what happened in Herne Bay was an accident. One drowning in a bath might be misfortune. The same circumstances three times was something else.

### 55
[ON SCREEN: card: "Witnesses: more than a hundred, according to accounts".]

So the jury heard all three stories. Accounts put the number of prosecution witnesses at more than a hundred: landladies, doctors, bank clerks, insurance men, an ironmonger, registrars. One by one, they laid out the same sequence: a marriage, a will or a policy, a bath, a death, a departure.

### 56
[ON SCREEN: card: Spilsbury in the witness box (text card), with the bath diagram behind.]

Spilsbury's evidence was the centre of it. Calm and certain, he took the jury through the measurements and the position of the body. A death like Bessie's, he said in effect, could not be explained by a fit. The bath from Herne Bay, it is reported, was there in court.

### 57
[ON SCREEN: archive document: Smith's handwritten note to Marshall Hall (Commons). Caption: Smith's note to his counsel.]

Smith did not give evidence. His decision survives in a handwritten note he passed to his counsel. Marshall Hall did what he could: he attacked the idea of system, and argued that suspicion multiplied by three was still only suspicion.

### 58
[ON SCREEN: card: "1 July 1915 — the jury retires".]

On the 1st of July, 1915, the jury retired. They were out for about twenty minutes. The verdict was guilty of the murder of Bessie Mundy. George Joseph Smith was sentenced to death.

### 59
[ON SCREEN: card: "Court of Criminal Appeal — Lord Reading".]

Marshall Hall appealed on the single ground that the evidence of system should never have been admitted. The Court of Criminal Appeal, under the Lord Chief Justice, Lord Reading, dismissed it. On the 13th of August, 1915, Smith was hanged at Maidstone Prison.

## LEGACY

### 60
[ON SCREEN: card: "Similar-fact evidence" with a timeline arrow: 1915 → later trials.]

The case is remembered for two firsts. In law, it became one of the first and best-known cases in which striking similarities between connected deaths were used to prove guilt. The principle of system evidence would be argued over in British courts for decades, and it was later used, and criticised, in other murder trials.

### 61
[ON SCREEN: card: Spilsbury — "brilliant, but fallible". (Sydney Smith)]

In forensic science, it made Bernard Spilsbury famous. He was not only telling a court what caused a death, but reconstructing how it happened. Later pathologists were more cautious about him. One called him very brilliant, but fallible, and very, very obstinate. Some of his later evidence has been questioned. The conviction in this case has never been overturned.

### 62
[ON SCREEN: card: names appear one at a time on dark background — Bessie Mundy · Alice Burnham · Margaret Lofty.]

And at the centre of it are three women who did nothing more than trust a husband. Bessie Mundy, in Herne Bay, in 1912. Alice Burnham, in Blackpool, in 1913. Margaret Lofty, in Highgate, in 1914. They are remembered here with respect.

### 63
[ON SCREEN: card: Charles Burnham and Joseph Crossley — "Two cuttings, one letter".]

Their story was not uncovered by a detective's hunch, or by a scientist alone. It began with a father who did not trust his son-in-law, and a boarding-house keeper in Blackpool who read a newspaper and decided to post a letter. This case file is closed.
