# The Crippen case — script

Working title: **Why The Captain's Telegram Became A Turning Point In The Crippen Case**
Format: one video, about 20 minutes. Narrator: Daniel (vidIQ). British spelling.
Each beat = one visual block. `[ON SCREEN: …]` is the image or graphic for that block; the

### 01

Have strong suspicions that Crippen London cellar murderer and accomplice are among saloon passengers. Mustache taken off growing beard. Accomplice dressed as boy. Manner and build undoubtedly a girl.

### 02
[ON SCREEN: SS Montrose photograph, slow push-in.]

Twenty-nine words, tapped out in Morse code from a ship heading west across the Atlantic, in July 1910. Within days, they would be on the front page of every newspaper in London. And no fugitive, it is said, had ever been caught like this before.

### 03
[ON SCREEN: map of the North Atlantic. A line from Antwerp toward Quebec; a second, faster line from Liverpool.]

On one side of the ocean, a quiet American doctor and a young woman dressed as a boy, travelling as father and son. On the other, a detective from Scotland Yard, on a faster ship, racing to reach Canada first.

### 04
[ON SCREEN: archive photograph of Hilldrop Crescent, Holloway.]

Behind them, in a house in north London, the police had found something under a cellar floor. And the man everyone was looking for had already been questioned once, and allowed to walk away.

This is the story of the Crippen case, and the first time wireless telegraphy helped to catch a man wanted for murder. This is Precinct Archive.

## ACT ONE — THE HOUSE ON HILLDROP CRESCENT

### 06
[ON SCREEN: portrait photograph of Hawley Harvey Crippen.]

Hawley Harvey Crippen was born in 1862 in Coldwater, Michigan. He trained in homeopathy, graduating from the Cleveland Homeopathic Medical College in 1884. He was a small, quiet, bespectacled man, and the people who knew him would later struggle to describe him as anything else.

### 07
[ON SCREEN: 1890s New York street scene; then a music hall poster.]

His first wife, Charlotte, died in 1892. Two years later, in New York, he married again. His second wife had been born Kunigunde Mackamotski, called herself Cora Turner, and wanted to be a singer. On stage, she would use another name: Belle Elmore.

### 08
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Cora Crippen, "Belle Elmore".]

In 1897 the couple moved to London, where Crippen was to manage the British office of a patent medicine company. There was a problem he could not get around. His American qualifications did not allow him to practise as a doctor in Britain. So he sold remedies instead, by post and over the counter, and moved from one firm to the next.

### 09
[ON SCREEN: Edwardian music hall interior; playbills.]

Cora threw herself into the music hall. Her stage career was modest, but her social life was not. She became a familiar face among the variety performers of the day, and she joined the Music Hall Ladies' Guild. Her friends were loyal, loud and observant. Hold on to that, because it is the reason this story was ever told.

### 10
[ON SCREEN: typewriter and office, early 1900s.]

In 1900, at the Drouet Institute for the Deaf, Crippen hired a young typist named Ethel Le Neve. She was seventeen, from Diss in Norfolk, and the daughter of a railway clerk. By 1905, according to the sources, the two were having an affair. By 1908, she was his mistress.

### 11
[ON SCREEN: street view of Hilldrop Crescent; house number 39 highlighted.]

By then the Crippens were living at number 39 Hilldrop Crescent, off Camden Road in Holloway. It was a large house for a man on a modest income, and they took in lodgers to help pay for it. By most accounts the marriage was not a happy one. Each of them, it is reported, had found someone else.

### 12
[ON SCREEN: candlelit dinner table, 1910. Date caption: 31 January 1910.]

On Monday, the 31st of January, 1910, the Crippens gave a small dinner party at Hilldrop Crescent. Two guests from the music hall world came to the house. They left in the early hours of the morning. They were among the last people known to have seen Cora Crippen alive.

### 13
[ON SCREEN: the front door of number 39, closed. Slow zoom.]

In the days that followed, Cora did not appear at the Guild. Crippen sent word that she had been called away to America, to see a sick relative. Then he said she had fallen ill there herself. Then, in March, he announced that she had died in California, and that her body had been cremated.

### 14
[ON SCREEN: newspaper notice of a death; a brooch and a fur.]

Something else was happening at the same time. Ethel Le Neve moved into Hilldrop Crescent. And she was seen in public wearing Cora's furs and Cora's jewellery, including at a dinner given by the Guild, where Cora's friends could see exactly what she had on.

### 15
[ON SCREEN: portrait of Kate Williams, "Vulcana"; photograph of Lil Hawthorne.]

Cora's friends did not believe him. Among them was the strongwoman Kate Williams, who performed as Vulcana, and who went to the police. The police did little at first. Then the actress Lil Hawthorne and her husband, John Nash, came back from a tour of America, where they had heard nothing about any death. Nash knew a senior officer at Scotland Yard, Superintendent Frank Froest, and he pressed him to look into it.

### 16
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Chief Inspector Walter Dew.]

The case went to Chief Inspector Walter Dew. He was a detective of long experience. In 1888 he had been a young constable in Whitechapel during the Ripper murders. By 1910 he had more than a hundred commendations to his name.

### 17
[ON SCREEN: Edwardian office; a desk with a notepad. Date caption: 8 July 1910.]

On the 8th of July, 1910, Dew went to see Crippen at his office. And Crippen did something unexpected. He admitted that he had lied. Cora was not dead, he said. She had left him, for an American music hall performer named Bruce Miller, and gone abroad. He had invented her death to spare himself the embarrassment.

### 18
[ON SCREEN: the cellar door at Hilldrop Crescent; torchlight effect.]

Crippen went with Dew to Hilldrop Crescent and let him look around, cellar included. Dew found nothing. By his own account he left reasonably satisfied. A wife who had left her husband was not a crime. Crippen agreed to help place a notice to find her.

### 19
[ON SCREEN: an empty office chair; a hat left on a hook.]

A few days later, Dew went back with a few routine questions. Crippen was gone. So was Ethel Le Neve. A man who had just told the police a credible story had run, and in running he had changed everything. The police went back to the house.

## ACT TWO — THE CELLAR AND THE SHIP

### 20
[ON SCREEN: the coal cellar, dark brick floor. Date caption: 13 July 1910.]

They searched Hilldrop Crescent three more times. On the fourth search, on the 12th and 13th of July, Dew turned his attention to the coal cellar. Some of the bricks in the floor were loose. Beneath them, in a layer of lime and clay, officers found human remains.

### 21
[ON SCREEN: muted, abstract — evidence tags on a table. No remains shown.]

What they found was partial. The head, the limbs and the skeleton were never recovered. With the remains were hair curlers, strands of bleached hair, and a fragment of a man's pyjama jacket bearing a maker's label. A missing person inquiry had become a murder investigation.

### 22
[ON SCREEN: laboratory glassware, early twentieth century; photograph of William Willcox.]

The Home Office analyst, William Willcox, examined the remains and found hyoscine, a powerful sedative. Records showed that Crippen had bought hyoscine from a chemist in January, shortly before Cora was last seen. On the 16th of July, warrants were issued for the arrest of Crippen and Le Neve.

### 23
[ON SCREEN: front pages, July 1910 — "Wanted" photographs of Crippen and Le Neve.]

The story went straight onto the front pages. Their photographs were printed under headlines about the North London cellar murder. Every reader in Britain was now looking for a small man with a moustache and gold-rimmed glasses, and a young woman with him.

### 24
[ON SCREEN: map — London to Brussels, Brussels to Antwerp.]

But Crippen was no longer in Britain. The day after Dew's first visit, he and Ethel had crossed to Belgium. They spent a night in a Brussels hotel, then went to the port of Antwerp. On the way, he shaved off his moustache. Ethel cut her hair and put on a boy's suit.

### 25
[ON SCREEN: photograph of SS Montrose at the dockside. Date caption: 20 July 1910.]

On the 20th of July, they boarded the Canadian Pacific liner Montrose, bound for Quebec. They travelled in second-class saloon accommodation, as a father and son: Mr John Robinson and his boy.

### 26
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Captain Henry George Kendall.]

The captain of the Montrose was Henry George Kendall. He was thirty-six, a career seaman, and he read the newspapers. He had also worked alongside Guglielmo Marconi on ship-to-shore radio before taking his first command. That detail matters.

### 27
[ON SCREEN: deck of an Edwardian liner; two figures at the rail, silhouetted.]

Kendall noticed Mr Robinson. In the captain's own later account, the man had the mark of spectacles on his nose but wore none, and the skin above his lip suggested a moustache recently removed. He noticed the son, too. The boy's trousers were held together with safety pins, and the way the young passenger moved struck the captain as unmistakably feminine. Kendall reportedly also saw the two holding hands, which seemed an odd thing for a father and a teenage son to do.

### 28
[ON SCREEN: wanted photograph of Crippen; Kendall's hand masking the moustache.]

So he tested the idea. According to accounts, he took one of the newspaper photographs and covered the moustache with his hand. And he tried a small trick: he called out to "Mr Robinson", and the man did not respond at once, as if it were not his name.

### 29
[ON SCREEN: Marconi wireless apparatus aboard ship; a telegraphist at the key.]

Here is the turning point. The Montrose carried a Marconi wireless set, and a telegraphist named Lawrence Hughes. The set had a limited range, and the ship would soon be beyond reach of the transmitters on land. Kendall wrote out a message and had Hughes send it to the line's office in Liverpool.

### 30
[ON SCREEN: the telegram text again, full screen.]

Have strong suspicions that Crippen London cellar murderer and accomplice are among saloon passengers. Mustache taken off growing beard. Accomplice dressed as boy. Manner and build undoubtedly a girl.

### 31
[ON SCREEN: Scotland Yard exterior, 1910.]

The message was passed to Scotland Yard. It is widely described as the first time that wireless telegraphy was used to help catch a criminal. And it put Walter Dew in front of a simple calculation.

### 32
[ON SCREEN: map — Montrose's slow line across the Atlantic; Laurentic leaving Liverpool.]

The Montrose was a steady ship, not a fast one. She was already days out into the Atlantic. But the White Star liner Laurentic, sailing from Liverpool, was quicker. If Dew could get aboard her, he might reach Canada first. He went to Liverpool and sailed.

### 33
[ON SCREEN: newspaper headlines, late July 1910 — "Race across the Atlantic".]

What happened next was unusual for another reason. Kendall kept sending reports as long as he could, and the newspapers learned of the chase. For days, readers in Britain followed a pursuit that the man being pursued knew nothing about. On board the Montrose, Crippen, by the accounts that survive, read, walked the deck, and remarked on the marvel of the wireless.

### 34
[ON SCREEN: two ships on the map, the lines converging on the St Lawrence.]

Two ships, one ocean, and a gap closing by the hour. Could Dew reach Quebec first?

## ACT THREE — FATHER POINT

### 35
[ON SCREEN: photograph of the Laurentic; date caption: 29 July 1910.]

He could. By his own account, Dew landed in Canada on the 29th of July, two days before the Montrose was due. He contacted the Canadian authorities, and together they made a plan.

### 36
[ON SCREEN: St Lawrence River, lighthouse at Father Point (Pointe-au-Père).]

Ships entering the St Lawrence stopped near Father Point, on the south shore, to take on a river pilot. A pilot coming aboard was routine. Nobody on deck would look twice.

### 37
[ON SCREEN: a small pilot boat approaching a liner. Date caption: 31 July 1910.]

On Sunday, the 31st of July, 1910, a pilot boat came out to the Montrose. Among the men who climbed aboard was Walter Dew, wearing a pilot's cap and uniform. With him was a Canadian police officer.

### 38
[ON SCREEN: the bridge of an Edwardian liner; three figures.]

Captain Kendall had arranged it. He invited Mr Robinson to come and meet the pilots. Crippen walked up, and Dew took off his cap.

### 39
[ON SCREEN: quotation on screen, typed. Photograph of Dew.]

"Good morning, Dr Crippen. Do you know me? I'm Chief Inspector Dew from Scotland Yard."

### 40
[ON SCREEN: quotation on screen. Photograph of Crippen.]

There was a pause. Then, according to the record, Crippen said: "Thank God it's over. The suspense has been too great. I couldn't stand it any longer." He held out his wrists for the handcuffs.

### 41
[ON SCREEN: a cabin door on a liner.]

Ethel Le Neve was arrested in her cabin soon afterwards. Because Canada was then a dominion within the British Empire, there was no long extradition battle. Both of them waived their rights, and they were brought back to England. They landed at Liverpool on the 27th of August, aboard the Megantic, to crowds that had been following the story for weeks.

---

## ACT FOUR — THE OLD BAILEY

### 42
[ON SCREEN: exterior of the Old Bailey, 1910. Date caption: 18 October 1910.]

Crippen's trial opened at the Old Bailey on the 18th of October, 1910, before the Lord Chief Justice, Lord Alverstone. It lasted four days.

### 43
[ON SCREEN: courtroom sketch; Crippen in the dock.]

The defence had a clear argument. Cora had left for America, as Crippen had said. And the remains, they suggested, could have been under the floor before the Crippens ever moved into the house. If that were true, then whatever lay in the cellar had nothing to do with him.

### 44
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Bernard Spilsbury.]

The prosecution answered with science. A young pathologist named Bernard Spilsbury examined a piece of skin from the remains and identified what he said was a scar, consistent with an operation Cora was known to have had. The defence argued that it was only a fold of tissue. The jury heard both.

### 45
[ON SCREEN: a label reading "Jones Bros", pyjama fabric, close-up.]

Then there was the pyjama jacket. A matching pair of pyjama trousers was found in Crippen's bedroom. A witness from the makers, Jones Brothers, said the material had not been sold before 1908, long after the Crippens moved into Hilldrop Crescent. Whoever was buried in that cellar had been buried there in Crippen's time.

### 46
[ON SCREEN: a chemist's register; the word "hyoscine".]

And there was the hyoscine. Crippen had bought it, shortly before his wife was last seen, and it was in the remains. He never gave an account of what he had done with it that the jury accepted.

### 47
[ON SCREEN: courtroom sketch — the jury. Caption: 27 minutes.]

The jury retired, and came back after twenty-seven minutes. Guilty. Throughout the trial, observers noted, Crippen showed little concern for himself, and a great deal for Ethel's reputation.

### 48
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Ethel Le Neve, 1910.]

Ethel Le Neve was tried separately, as an accessory after the fact. She was acquitted.

### 49
[ON SCREEN: Pentonville Prison exterior. Date caption: 23 November 1910.]

Hawley Harvey Crippen was hanged at Pentonville Prison at nine in the morning on Wednesday, the 23rd of November, 1910. At his request, a photograph of Ethel was buried with him. He never said why he killed his wife. Theories have been offered ever since. They remain theories.

## LEGACY

### 50
[ON SCREEN: Marconi wireless room; newspapers praising the capture.]

The newspapers called it the most sensational capture of the century. What made it sensational was not the detective work alone. It was a message that crossed the sea faster than the ship carrying the man it described. For the first time, a fugitive on the open ocean was no longer out of reach.

### 51
[ON SCREEN: Walter Dew in later life; cover of "I Caught Crippen".]

Walter Dew resigned from the police not long after the case. Years later he wrote a memoir called I Caught Crippen, and admitted in it that his memory of some details had faded. Henry Kendall kept his command, and four years later survived the sinking of the Empress of Ireland.

### 52
[ON SCREEN: Ethel Le Neve in later years, or a quiet Croydon street.]

Ethel Le Neve left for Canada, came back to London under another name, married, and raised two children in Croydon. She died in 1967.

### 53
[ON SCREEN: Michigan State University laboratory; a microscope slide.]

The case has not entirely rested. In 2007, a team led by the American forensic scientist David Foran reported that DNA from a century-old slide of the cellar remains did not match Cora's living relatives, and suggested the tissue was male. Others disputed the method and the sample. In 2009, the Criminal Cases Review Commission declined to refer the case to the Court of Appeal. The conviction stands.

### 54
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Cora Crippen, held still. Soft fade.]

And behind all of it was a woman whose name is often left until last. Cora Crippen, Belle Elmore, a music hall singer whose friends refused to accept that she had simply gone away. If they had stayed quiet, no one would have asked the questions. She is remembered here with respect.

[ON SCREEN: telegram form, the words fading. Title card: PRECINCT ARCHIVE.]
