## Description

27 September 1927. An Essex village constable, PC George Gutteridge, stops a car on a dark lane near Stapleford Abbotts and takes out his notebook. At dawn a postman finds him at the roadside, shot four times, still holding his pencil. In the stolen car, abandoned in Brixton, Scotland Yard finds one empty army cartridge case.

This is the full story of the Gutteridge murder: the old Mark IV cartridge from Woolwich Arsenal, the four-month hunt that led to Frederick Guy Browne's garage at Clapham Junction and William Henry Kennedy's arrest in Liverpool, and the Old Bailey trial of April 1928 at which gun experts matched two tiny marks on a revolver's breech shield to the cap of that cartridge, and the jury were handed a microscope to see for themselves.

It also weighs two things most retellings skip: the famous legend that the constable's eyes were shot because a dead man's retina was believed to keep the killer's image (the trial record does not say so), and the legal point that Kennedy's statement was not evidence against Browne, which is why the "gun print" mattered so much.

Where the record is uncertain, this video says so. The account of the shooting comes from one man's statement, and later doubts about who fired are presented as opinions.
