## Who I am talking to

One person: an adult who likes true crime but is tired of gore and speculation, and who
enjoys learning *how* a case was solved — the first fingerprint conviction, the first wireless
arrest, the first DNA match. They watch in the evening or before bed, want a whole case told
properly in one sitting, and trust a narrator who says "reportedly" when the record is
thin. **(assumed — confirm or rewrite)**

## How I actually talk

Calm, documentary narration in the third person. Faceless: a voiceover over a music bed and
archive-style visuals. Never "I", almost never "you". The narrator is the keeper of the
file, not a character in the video.

**Cold open, present tense, timestamp first.** Part 1 starts with date, time and place, then
one ordinary person walking into the moment:
> Monday, the 27th of March, 1905. Half past eight in the morning, Deptford High Street in
> Southeast London. A 16 year old named William Jones arrives for work at Chapman's Oil and
> Colour Shop.

**Short declarative beats at the tense moments.**
> Jones knocks. Nobody answers. Through the window, he can see chairs knocked over.

**A flash-forward that states what is at stake, then the "first" the series is about.**
> Within days, Scotland Yard will have a clue, a greasy smudge on a metal cashbox. By the end
> of May, two young men will be dead because of it. No British jury had ever convicted anyone
> of murder on the strength of a fingerprint.

**Then the brand line, around 0:60:** "This is Precinct Archive."

**Context is explained as a mechanism, not decoration.** The narrator flags what matters:
> There was one more habit in that shop that matters to this story. Every Monday morning,
> George Chapman came by to collect the previous week's takings.

**The narrator separates fact from inference out loud.** This is the voice's signature:
> A mask is worn by someone who thinks he might be recognized. That is an inference, not a
> fact from the file, but it shaped how the newspapers described the crime.

**Turns inside the video.** The old series used these lines to end each part. In one long video
they become the hinges between acts, and the next act starts straight away with no recap:
- a planted warning: "Hold on to that, because the identifications would not hold up when it
  mattered."
- one open question: "Could he reach Quebec first?"

**The ending:** the legacy ("remembered for two firsts"), the victims named with respect, then
the fixed sign-off: "This case file is closed. New case files arrive every day on Precinct
Archive."

## Words I never use

- Sensational adjectives: shocking, horrifying, gruesome, chilling, insane, twisted, evil,
  monster, sick
- Hype: "you won't believe", "the truth will shock you", "game-changer", "unlock", "dive in",
  "let's get into it", "buckle up"
- Creator talk: "hey guys", "welcome back", "smash that like", "before we start, subscribe"
- Speculation presented as fact: "clearly", "obviously he", "we all know"
- Graphic detail beyond what the record needs. "killed by repeated blows to the head" is the
  ceiling.

## Words I do use

- Hedges that track the source: "reportedly", "according to reports", "accounts put the sum
  at", "is widely described as"
- "That is an inference, not a fact from the file"
- "There was one more [thing] that matters to this story"
- "Hold on to that"
- "the file", "the case file", "the inquiry", "the record"
- "became a turning point" (the title spine)
- Full dates in British order: "the 16th of July", "Monday, the 27th of March, 1905"
- Ages and jobs as identifiers: "a 27-year-old baker from the nearby village of Littlethorpe"
- Spelling: British in narration (neighbourhood, colour). The transcripts mix US and British
  forms; standardise on British. **(assumed)**

## What I will not claim

- Nothing that is not in the historical record. Contested details get "reportedly" or
  "according to reports", every time.
- No motive or inner thought the sources do not give. Write what the person did, not what
  they felt.
- No new theories about a closed case, and no suggestion that a conviction was wrong unless
  a court or official review said so.
- No exact figures without a source. Money and numbers get "accounts put it at about".
- Victims are never the hook and never a punchline. They are named and remembered "with
  respect" in the close.

## My format

- **One case, one video.** No parts, no series. The whole closed case is told in a single
  long video. (The first nine uploads were three-part series; that format is dropped.)
- **Length:** 8 to 12 minutes, about 150 words a minute, so 1,200–1,800 words of narration.
  **(assumed — confirm)**
- **Structure:**
  1. Cold open (0:00–1:00): date, time and place, the scene, a flash-forward, and the "first".
  2. "This is Precinct Archive."
  3. Act 1, the crime and the setting. Ends on a planted warning ("Hold on to that…").
  4. Act 2, the investigation. Ends on an open question.
  5. Act 3, the break, the arrest and the trial.
  6. The legacy, the victims named with respect, and the sign-off.
  Put a turn about every 2–3 minutes so there's always an open question.
- **Titles:** "Why The [Specific Moment] Became A Turning Point In The [Case]", without
  "| Part N of 3". The moment is the one forensic first the case is remembered for.
- **Visuals:** faceless — archive photos, newspaper front pages, maps, documents, under a
  low music bed. No presenter on camera. **(assumed — confirm)**
- **Intro:** a cold open of about 60 seconds, then "This is Precinct Archive." No channel
  intro, no subscribe pitch at the start.
- **Close:** the fixed sign-off, once, at the end. No call to action anywhere in the middle.
- **No swearing.** Calm, slow, even. The tension comes from facts, not from delivery.

## Notes for the skills

- `hookscore.py` rewards "you" in the first six words (ADDRESS). This channel deliberately
  doesn't address the viewer, so expect ADDRESS to score low on good hooks. Don't add "you"
  just to raise the score. Judge on SPECIFICITY, STAKES and CURIOSITY.
- The strongest hook pattern here is **The Statistic / a first**: "No British jury had ever
  convicted anyone of murder on the strength of a fingerprint." Put that line earlier. In Part 1
  it arrives at 0:45, after three sentences of scene.
